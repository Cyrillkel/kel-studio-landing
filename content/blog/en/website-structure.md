A website's structure is its set of pages and the links between them. It determines where someone arrives from search or advertising, where they find the service they need and how they send an enquiry. Planning it before design helps: adding a missing section on paper is easier than reworking finished layouts and navigation.

A small business might need only a few pages. A company with different lines of work needs individual service pages, project examples and material for people still researching the subject. We begin [corporate website development](/services/corporate) with the tasks and page list. Below, we show how to prepare that list yourself using a hypothetical business and the KEL Studio website.

## First decide what visitors need to do

Write down the customer's main actions. For a service company, these might be requesting an estimate, booking a consultation or calling. For a store, they might be finding a product, choosing a variant and placing an order.

Then describe the situations in which people arrive at the site. One visitor already knows the company name and wants its phone number. Another is comparing contractors. A third searches for a specific service and knows nothing about you. Each needs a clear entry point and a next step.

For example, a hypothetical home renovation company offers cosmetic work, full renovations and renovations of newly built apartments. Someone looking for work on a new apartment needs the scope, examples and estimate process for that situation. On a general "Services" page, they would have to piece the answer together.

## How to turn services and search queries into pages

Start with what the business actually sells. Add the questions a manager gets before a purchase: what is included, what it costs, how long the work takes and what the customer needs to prepare.

Then match the list against search queries. The detailed process is covered in our article on [keyword research](/blog/semantic-core). For the structure, the important step is grouping queries by meaning and the visitor's expectations.

"Book a new-build apartment renovation" and "turnkey renovation of a new apartment" may belong on one service page. Someone searching for "where to start with renovating a new apartment" first needs an explanation of the stages. An article linking to the relevant service suits that need.

You can build a working table like this:

| Query group | Page | What the visitor needs to find |
|---|---|---|
| Turnkey apartment renovation | Service overview | Types of work, differences and links to details |
| New-build apartment renovation | Service page | Scope, examples, timing and an estimate request |
| Renovation cost | Prices | Starting points and factors affecting the estimate |
| Where to start a renovation | Article | The sequence of steps and preparation |

The table describes a hypothetical business. Your own pages depend on the services and the material you can prepare for each one.

## When a service needs its own page

A separate page makes sense when an offer has its own audience, content and terms. Check whether you can explain the scope, show a relevant example and answer the customer's questions.

If two planned pages differ only in the word order of their headings, first clarify their purpose. The queries may refer to one task and belong together. If the same person gets the same answer on both pages, maintaining both will be difficult.

At KEL Studio, for example, [landing pages](/services/landing) and [corporate websites](/services/corporate) are separate services. Their structure, scope of work and use cases differ. There is also an [all services page](/services), which helps visitors choose a direction.

A company with a single offer may only need a [landing page](/blog/what-is-a-landing-page). Build a multipage site around actual sections and tasks.

## An example of a corporate website structure

For the same hypothetical renovation company, a first version might look like this:

```text
/
|-- services/
|   |-- cosmetic-renovation/
|   |-- full-renovation/
|   `-- new-build-renovation/
|-- projects/
|   `-- apartment-example/
|-- prices/
|-- about/
|-- blog/
|   `-- renovation-planning/
`-- contacts/
```

This is a plan for discussion, with no required number of sections. If the portfolio has just one project, it could appear within a service page. If the company sells two straightforward offers, deep nesting may also be unnecessary.

Add a short note to every page: who needs it, which question it answers, what material it requires and where the visitor goes next. Those notes are enough to discuss the site's scope with a designer and developer.

The addresses in the plan are examples. Readable names help, but links also need to support the hierarchy: from the service list to individual services, from a case study to the relevant service, and from an article to the next useful resource.

## Menus and internal links

Put the main areas in the menu. Details can appear on section pages, while administrative information can live in the footer. This makes it easier for visitors to choose a route, especially on a phone.

Google's [guidance on ecommerce site structure](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/help-google-understand-your-ecommerce-site-structure) describes the connections between menus, categories and product pages. The search engine uses links between pages to find content and understand its place on the site. For a service website, we apply the same principle of connected navigation.

Check a few routes:

- the home page links to the main services;
- a service page leads to relevant work examples and an enquiry form;
- an article links to a resource or service that helps with the next step;
- a detailed page provides a way back to its parent section.

Name links after their content: "Online store development", "Renovation costs", "Apartment projects". Google also recommends [meaningful link text and links with an address in the HTML](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable). This helps visitors know where a link will take them.

A sitemap.xml file helps with page discovery, but navigation still needs to provide a familiar route through the site. Technical sitemaps and indexing are covered in a [separate article](/blog/sitemap-robots-txt).

## What to prepare within each page

Once the section list is ready, outline the content of typical pages. A service page should answer the visitor's question and provide enough information to make an enquiry.

Start with five questions:

1. Does the first screen explain the offer and who it suits?
2. Are the scope and the deliverables described?
3. Are there relevant examples, photographs or an explanation of the process?
4. Is there a price or an estimate process, a timeframe and conditions for starting?
5. Is there a clear way to get in touch that works on a phone?

The details depend on the service. A complex project needs an explanation of stages and constraints. A standard service may need to show terms and available booking times quickly. At this point, you can commission [prototyping and web design](/services/design) with an agreed page list and materials.

## How KEL Studio's website is structured

Our home page introduces the service areas and shows prices, projects and the process. Visitors can move from it to a detailed page for the service they need. The services section brings the different areas together, while a separate prices page helps compare the basic options.

The blog answers questions people have before commissioning work. For example, the [overview of ways to create a website](/blog/how-to-create-a-website) helps with choosing a format, while the article on [website CMS options](/blog/website-cms) covers content management. Articles link to the relevant services. Service pages also display related articles.

The Russian and English versions have their own addresses. The language switch opens the matching page in the other language. This required planning address pairs and links between pages so visitors stay in the relevant section after switching.

This example illustrates how sections can connect. A renovation company's menu, a store's menu and a development studio's menu will have different contents.

## Reviewing the website structure before launch

Open the plan and follow it as a new customer. Start from a specific service, then from the home page and from an article. For each entry point, check whether the information is sufficient and the next step is available.

Use this short checklist for the main website structure requirements:

- every page has a purpose and prepared material;
- important pages are reachable through internal links;
- section names make sense to someone unfamiliar with the company;
- two pages do not repeat the same answer without a reason;
- a service leads to an enquiry and an article leads to related material;
- the menu is easy to open and scroll on a phone;
- new services and articles have an appropriate section to go into.

If the website is already live and page addresses will change, prepare a list of old and new URLs. Set up the corresponding redirects for moved content and update internal links. This is a separate migration task to include in the scope of work.

## What to hand to the developer

For an initial estimate, prepare the page tree, keyword table and feature list. Beside every page, note whether its text and images are ready. Say who will publish changes after launch: that affects the [choice of CMS](/blog/website-cms).
